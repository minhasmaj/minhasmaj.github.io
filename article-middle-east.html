The Middle East Power Triangle: US, Iran & Israel

An Analytical Overview
Dr. Minhas Majeed Khan  ·  May 2026

A Region Defined by Competing Interests

The Middle East has long been the world's most consequential geopolitical arena. At its centre sits a triangle of powers — the United States, Iran, and Israel — each with legitimate security concerns, each with a grand strategy, and each contributing, in its own way, to a cycle of tension that has now escalated into open conflict. Understanding this triangle requires setting aside simplified narratives of good and evil, and examining instead the interests, fears, and miscalculations that have brought the region to its current state.

Historical Roots

The rivalry between these three powers is not a recent phenomenon. Its modern origins trace back to 1979, when Iran's Islamic Revolution ended decades of close US-Iranian relations and severed Iran's ties with Israel entirely. Before 1979, Israel and Iran had cooperative relations under the Shah, viewing Arab states as their shared strategic concern.

Post-revolution, Iran positioned itself as the principal ideological challenger to both American influence and Israeli presence in the region, a posture it has maintained for over four decades. The United States, meanwhile, deepened its security commitment to Israel, a relationship that has remained bipartisan across administrations regardless of periodic diplomatic tensions.

The Three Players

The United States

Washington's Middle East policy has been shaped by two consistent objectives: ensuring Israel's security and preventing any hostile power from dominating the region's energy resources and strategic waterways. The US has pursued these goals through a combination of military presence, arms supply, sanctions, and diplomacy.

The landmark 2015 Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA) represented Washington's most significant attempt at diplomatic engagement with Tehran, curbing Iran's nuclear programme in exchange for sanctions relief. The Trump administration unilaterally withdrew from the agreement in 2018, initiating a cycle of "maximum pressure" and "maximum resistance" that brought both sides to the edge of direct confrontation.

Upon returning to office in January 2025, President Trump restored maximum pressure while simultaneously initiating the first direct US-Iran nuclear talks since the JCPOA withdrawal. Those negotiations ultimately failed in February 2026, with the Omani mediator indicating significant Iranian concessions had been offered before talks collapsed.

Iran

Iran's foreign policy since 1979 has been shaped by genuine security pressures: US-backed regime change in 1953, decades of sanctions, international isolation, and the memory of a devastating eight-year war with Iraq that the West largely supported. Its response has been the construction of a regional network of allied and proxy forces — Hezbollah in Lebanon, the Houthis in Yemen, Shia militias in Iraq and Syria, and Hamas in Gaza — collectively known as the Axis of Resistance.

From Tehran's perspective, this network is a defensive perimeter. From Washington and Jerusalem's perspective, it is an aggressive tool of regional destabilisation. Both readings seem to contain truth.

Iran's nuclear programme has been the most explosive flashpoint. Tehran insists it is for civilian purposes. Western powers, backed by intelligence assessments, have long suspected otherwise, though the US Defense Intelligence Agency's own 2025 assessment found no conclusive evidence of an active nuclear weapons programme.

Israel

Israel operates under a security doctrine forged by its history as a small state surrounded by adversaries. Its doctrine of pre-emption — acting to prevent threats before they materialise — has guided decades of military strategy. Israel has conducted airstrikes on nuclear facilities in Iraq (1981), Syria (2007), and repeatedly targeted Iranian assets in Syria throughout the 2010s and 2020s.

The 2023 Hamas attack of October 7, in which some 1,200 Israelis were killed and approximately 250 taken hostage, fundamentally altered Israel's strategic calculus. It launched a comprehensive military campaign that, by the end of 2024, had significantly weakened Hamas in Gaza, decapitated Hezbollah's leadership in Lebanon, and contributed to the fall of Syria's Assad regime, Iran's most important state ally.

The Escalation Timeline: 2023–2026

The shift from proxy conflict to direct confrontation has been rapid:

  • October 2023 — Hamas launches a large-scale attack on Israel, triggering the Gaza war and a broader regional mobilisation of Iran's Axis of Resistance.
  • April 2024 — Iran launches its first-ever direct missile strike on Israel in retaliation for Israel's bombing of the Iranian consulate in Damascus, killing senior Iranian military officials. Israel responds with a limited strike on Iranian air defences.
  • October 2024 — Iran launches approximately 180 ballistic missiles at Israel. Israel retaliates with its largest-ever direct strike on Iran, targeting air defence systems and missile production facilities.
  • June 2025 — Israel launches a surprise attack on Iranian military and nuclear facilities, triggering the "Twelve-Day War." The United States directly intervenes offensively for the first time, striking three Iranian nuclear sites with B-2 bombers and cruise missiles. A ceasefire follows, though no formal agreement is signed.
  • January 2026 — Mass protests erupt across Iran, the largest since 1979, driven by economic collapse and infrastructure failure. The Iranian government suppresses them with force, killing thousands of civilians.
  • February 28, 2026 — The US and Israel launch Operation Epic Fury and Operation Roaring Lion, coordinated strikes targeting Iran's nuclear programme, ballistic missile infrastructure, and military leadership, including Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, who is killed. His son, Mojtaba Khamenei, is appointed as successor.
  • March–May 2026 — Iran retaliates by striking US military facilities across the region, targeting all six Gulf Cooperation Council states, and threatening closure of the Strait of Hormuz, through which approximately 20 percent of the world's oil supply passes. The conflict remains ongoing.

The Broader Consequences

For the Region

The smaller states of the Middle East — Lebanon, Yemen, Syria, Iraq, and the Palestinian territories — have borne a disproportionate cost. They did not initiate this power struggle, but their populations, infrastructure, and sovereignty have been its primary casualties.

The Gulf states find themselves in a particularly difficult position. Having sought to deepen diplomatic ties with both the US and Iran in recent years, they are now absorbing Iranian retaliatory strikes as a consequence of a war they neither wanted nor chose.

For Global Energy Markets

The International Energy Agency has warned of significant volatility. The Strait of Hormuz, the world's most critical oil chokepoint, has become a focal point of the conflict. Any sustained disruption would send global energy prices sharply higher, with consequences felt far beyond the Middle East, particularly in energy-importing developing economies.

For International Law and Multilateralism

Critics, including international law experts, have described the February 2026 US strikes as illegal under US law and a violation of Iranian sovereignty under international law. The United Nations Secretary-General Antonio Guterres condemned the strikes. The UN Security Council passed a resolution condemning Iran's retaliatory strikes on Gulf states. Neither resolution has changed the trajectory of the conflict.

For China and Russia's Role

China has been a critical silent stakeholder throughout. It purchased more than 80 percent of Iran's exported crude oil in 2025, making Iran integral to its energy security despite international sanctions. China's 2023 brokering of the Saudi-Iran rapprochement demonstrated that it has both the will and the leverage to play a diplomatic role in the region, a role the US once held exclusively.

Russia has deepened its ties with Iran throughout this period. Both countries view American military dominance in the region as a direct threat to their own strategic interests.

The Fundamental Questions

On Iran's nuclear programme: The IAEA has confirmed damage to Iran's Natanz facility from the June 2025 war, but has found no evidence of an active nuclear weapons programme. Whether Iran reconstitutes its nuclear capabilities — and whether it does so overtly this time — is the defining question of regional security.

On the possibility of diplomacy: Iranian officials have indicated a willingness to negotiate under conditions of mutual respect. American officials have publicly stated a preference for diplomacy. Yet both sides have defined their red lines in ways that leave little overlap. The collapse of the February 2026 Oman talks despite significant Iranian concessions suggests structural barriers that goodwill alone cannot overcome.

On who bears the cost: The civilians of Iran, Gaza, Lebanon, Yemen, and the Gulf states continue to pay the heaviest price for decisions made by governments and militaries far removed from the consequences of those decisions. This asymmetry between those who choose conflict and those who suffer it is, ultimately, the most important and least discussed dimension of the Middle East power struggle.

Conclusion

The US-Iran-Israel triangle is not a simple story of aggressor and victim. It is a story of three states — each with real security fears, each with strategic doctrines that are rational from their own vantage point, and each with a history of miscalculation — locked in a dynamic that has now crossed the threshold from managed tension into open war.

What is clear is that military force alone will not resolve the underlying tensions. Iran's nuclear ambitions, Israel's security requirements, and America's regional interests are not inherently irreconcilable, but reconciling them requires the kind of sustained, principled diplomacy that has been repeatedly sacrificed to short-term political calculations.

The Strait of Hormuz remains open, for now. The question is whether the parties involved, and the international community watching, can find the will to act before the cost of inaction becomes truly irreversible.
